  second-period benefits are expectations conditional on first-period realisations; development is
  irreversible, preservation is not; risk neutrality throughout.
  Result (p. 314, restated p. 315): if development involves an irreversible transformation (a loss in
  perpetuity of preservation benefits) and information realised in period 1 changes expectations for
  period 2, then "net benefits from developing the area are reduced and, broadly speaking, less of the
  area should be developed". The authors call the source of this reduction a "quasi-option value",
  acting in the same direction as risk aversion though derived without it (p. 315).
  Interpretation (p. 319): irreversibility does not mean an action should not be taken; its effect is
  to reduce the benefits, which are then balanced against costs in the usual way. The point is
  generalised beyond environment: the expected benefits of an irreversible decision should be adjusted
  for the loss of options it entails. Applications noted: persistent pollutants (DDT, lead) and
  cumulative CO2 accumulation.

fit_note: |
  The formal result is that, under uncertainty with the prospect of information, an irreversible option
  should be charged an additional cost (quasi-option value) relative to a reversible one; that is
  "extra weight" for irreversibility under uncertainty. Three qualifications for the register:
  (1) the result needs expected learning, not uncertainty alone; (2) it is a downward adjustment of
  benefits, not a prohibition or lexical priority; (3) it is symmetric: whichever choice is
  irreversible (development, or sunk-cost protection) earns the adjustment, so it does not by itself
  favour preservation in every case (Pindyck 2006, Section 3). Within those limits the claim is a fair
  statement of the theorem.
