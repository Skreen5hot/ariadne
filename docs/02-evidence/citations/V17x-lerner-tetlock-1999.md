  Full text (author-hosted PDF, fetched with WebFetch and converted with pdftotext). Narrative review of two
  decades of experimental research on accountability; no meta-analysis, no new data.
  Definition (p. 255): accountability is the implicit or explicit expectation that one may be called on to justify
  one's beliefs, feelings and actions to others; it is 'a mistake ... to view accountability as a unitary
  phenomenon' (p. 255); at least eight kinds have been studied.
  Known vs unknown audience (pp. 256-257): when audience views are known before forming an opinion, people shift
  attitudes toward the audience (conformity), including in highly involving contexts (Tetlock 1983a; Tetlock et
  al. 1989; Pennington & Schlenker; Brockner et al. 1981; Brief et al. 1991). When views are unknown, people
  engage in preemptive self-criticism: more integratively complex thinking, tolerance of evaluative
  inconsistency, recognition of trade-offs (p. 257).
  Pre- vs post-decisional (p. 257): post-decisional accountability prompts defensive bolstering and amplifies
  sunk-cost escalation; pre-decisional accountability attenuates it.
  Process vs outcome accountability (p. 258): process accountability improves judgment; outcome accountability
  can fail or backfire.
  Synthesis (p. 259): self-critical, effortful thinking is most likely when people learn before forming opinions
  that they are accountable to an audience whose views are unknown, that is interested in accuracy and in
  process, is well informed, and has a legitimate reason to inquire; even then effects 'are highly variable
  across judgment tasks and dependent variables, sometimes improving, sometimes having no effect on, and
  sometimes degrading judgment and choice'. Table 1 (pp. 260-262) tabulates bias attenuation (e.g.
  overattribution to dispositions, overconfidence, primacy and recency effects, anchoring), no effect, and
  amplification. Integrative conclusions (p. 263): pre-decisional accountability to an unknown audience
  attenuates bias when the bias stems from lack of self-critical attention and needs no special training to fix;
  it amplifies bias when the bias comes from using normatively proscribed information.
  Conclusions (pp. 270-271): the review 'underscores the falsity of the conventional wisdom ... that
  accountability is a cognitive or social panacea'; only specialised subtypes increase cognitive effort, more
  effort is not inherently beneficial, and lab effects are presumably minimal estimates of everyday effects.
  Design: narrative review. Sample: none. Effect: no pooled effect sizes.
