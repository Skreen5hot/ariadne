finding: |
  The work and chapter exist as cited. The PDF opened is the second edition (OUP 2011; copyright page: J. M. Finnis 2011, "First published 2011 (first edition 1980)", ISBNs above). Chapter V begins on p. 100 and has sections V.1-V.10 (archive.org catalogue record and the PDF agree): V.1 the good of practical reasonableness structures our pursuit of goods; V.2 a coherent plan of life (p. 103); V.3 no arbitrary preferences amongst values (p. 105); V.4 no arbitrary preferences amongst persons; V.5 detachment and commitment; V.6 the (limited) relevance of consequences; V.7 respect for every basic value in every act; V.8 the requirements of the common good; V.9 following one's conscience; V.10 the product of these requirements: morality.
  "Many legitimate paths": V.1, p. 100: each of the seven basic goods "can be participated in, and promoted, in an inexhaustible variety of ways and with an inexhaustible variety of combinations of emphasis, concentration, and specialization"; choice of commitments "effectively rules out many alternative reasonable or possible commitments". V.3, p. 106: "It is quite reasonable for many people to choose not to commit themselves to any real pursuit of knowledge", and it is unreasonable for scholar-statesmen or scholar-parents to demand that others conform to their own standards of excellence. V.4, p. 108: the impartiality heuristic "permits one to be impartial, too, among inexhaustibly many of the life-plans that differing individuals may choose". V.6, p. 113: love of God may be expressed in "any of the inexhaustibly many life-plans which conform to the requirements set out in this chapter".
  "Not all equally good": Finnis excludes plans rather than ranking the reasonable ones. V.2, p. 104: it is unreasonable to live from moment to moment or just drift, and irrational to attend only to definable projects; from the standpoint of one's death "many sorts of choices would be irrational, a waste of opportunities, meaningless, a failure, a shame". V.3, p. 105: a commitment is rational only if based on assessment of capacities, circumstances and tastes, and unreasonable if based on devaluing any basic good or overvaluing instrumental goods; it is "stupid or arbitrary" to act as if goods one lacks taste for "were not real forms of good". V.6, p. 112: the basic goods are "equally basic", none "objectively more important than any of the others", so "they are incommensurable" and any calculus pretending to commensurate them is irrational. Finnis therefore does NOT rank reasonable life plans against one another; within the space of plans meeting the nine requirements there is no scale on which one is better than another.

fit_note: |
  First half ("many legitimate paths"): fully supported; Finnis says so repeatedly and in strong terms (inexhaustibly many reasonable life-plans).
  Second half ("not all equally good"): supported only in the sense that practical reasonableness excludes some plans as unreasonable (drifting, devaluing a basic good, preferring trash to art, plans involving intentional killing, fraud, etc. under V.7). It is NOT supported in the sense of a ranking among legitimate paths: Finnis holds the basic goods incommensurable (p. 112) and treats the reasonable plans as objects of impartiality, not as better and worse. If the claim means "some paths are illegitimate", Finnis fits; if it means "among the legitimate paths some are better than others", Finnis is the wrong source and arguably contradicts it.

## Notes
The text was read from the PDF that WebFetch retrieved (converted locally with pdftotext; page numbers taken from the running headers). The 2011 second edition reproduces the 1980 text with a new postscript; the archive.org record for a separate scan also places chapter V at p. 100, so the 1980 and 2011 paginations appear to coincide for this chapter, but the 1980 printing was not itself opened. The lawprof.co summary was paywalled after the first paragraph and the OUP product page returned an empty body. If the register wants a source that does rank legitimate paths (the second reading of the claim), Finnis is not it; Raz's perfectionism (The Morality of Freedom, chs. 12 and 14) or a virtue-ethical account would fit that reading better, but neither was opened for this purpose.
