fit_note: |
  Heuer argues precisely the claim - keeping competing explanations open, and seeking to disprove rather than
  confirm, improves analysis - and designs ACH to institutionalise it. But the book supplies no controlled evidence
  for it, and the 'proven' label is unsupported within the text. The controlled tests of ACH run since 1999 (listed
  under replication) are mixed: two small early studies found partial benefits (Folker 2000 on one of two scenarios,
  N=26; Lehner et al. 2008 only for participants without analytic experience, N=24), while later and larger studies
  found no reduction in confirmation bias (Whitesmith 2019; Maegherman et al. 2020), no accuracy gain and worse
  within-analyst consistency (Dhami, Belton & Mandel 2019, N=50), and a 2024 critical review of six experiments
  concludes ACH has little to no overall benefit and may harm judgment. Dhami et al. (2019) did find that
  ACH-trained analysts more often considered all alternative hypotheses - the 'holding open' behaviour - but this
  did not improve accuracy. The source therefore argues the claim; the evidence base neither confirms nor clearly
  refutes 'holding open improves analysis' in the process sense, and does not support the stronger outcome reading
  that the ACH procedure improves analytic accuracy.

## Notes
- The claim as worded is ambiguous between a process reading (analysts consider more alternatives) and an outcome
  reading (judgments get better). Dhami et al. (2019) support the process reading (ACH group considered all
  hypotheses more often) and not the outcome reading (no accuracy difference). Heuer's own text claims both.
- 'Argued only' is assigned because the cited source is a practitioner argument with no test of the claim; the
  ACH experiments are tests of a procedure built on the claim, and they are mixed, so they do not raise the grade.
  If the ratifier prefers to treat them as direct tests, the appropriate replication status is 'mixed' and the
  grade should still not exceed Argued only.
