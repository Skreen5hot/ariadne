  Both works exist as cited. The PDF opened is headed "Isaiah Berlin, 'Two Concepts of Liberty', Four Essays On Liberty (Oxford University Press, 1969), p. 118-172" and contains the full lecture in eight sections. Wikipedia confirms the lecture date (31 October 1958), the Clarendon pamphlet, and the 1969 and 2002 reprints.
  Section VIII, "The One and the Many" (the closing section): Berlin argues that the belief that all positive values must ultimately be compatible is false; that justice and generosity, public and private loyalties, the demands of genius and the claims of society can conflict violently; that "not all good things are compatible, still less all the ideals of mankind"; that conflicts of values may be "an intrinsic, irremovable element in human life"; and that we face "choices between ends equally ultimate, and claims equally absolute". Later in the same section: "human goals are many, not all of them commensurable, and in perpetual rivalry with one another", and "the ends of men are many, and not all of them are in principle compatible". Immediately before section VIII (end of VII) he says negative and positive liberty make "absolute claims" which cannot both be satisfied, yet each is "an ultimate value" with "an equal right" to count among the deepest interests of mankind.
  "The Pursuit of the Ideal" (NYRB, 17 March 1988; address at the Agnelli Prize ceremony, Turin): the NYRB page opened states that values may clash within a single individual and it does not follow that some must be true and others false; we are "doomed to choose, and every choice may entail an irreparable loss". archive.org confirms it is chapter 1 of The Crooked Timber of Humanity (John Murray, 1990).

fit_note: |
  Exact fit for both halves of the claim: genuine (ultimate, equally absolute) ends conflict, and the conflict does not show that one of them is unreal or false. Berlin is the standard citation for this position in political philosophy. Note that Berlin's stronger thesis (values are incommensurable and there is no single scale) goes beyond what the claim needs; the claim needs only conflict without unreality.
