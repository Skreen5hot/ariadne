  Abstract (p. 119) states four "replicable findings": (1) large correlations between the wealth

  larger in poor nations, with "the risk of unhappiness ... much higher for poor people";
  (3) economic growth in developed societies accompanied by little rise in SWB, and individual
  income increases with variable outcomes; (4) people who prize material goals are substantially
  less happy unless rich.
  Within nations (Table I, p. 123): mean r = 0.13 across 19 nations (Diener & Oishi 2000; range
  -0.02 to 0.38); US samples 0.12-0.18; retired professors 0.24; Indian village 0.22-0.35; poor
  areas of Calcutta 0.45. World Value Survey II, controlling nation means (p. 126): income r = 0.13
  with life satisfaction, 0.13 with positive affect, -0.10 with negative affect.
  Poverty risk (Tables II-III, pp. 124-127): across nations 17 percentage points more of the
  wealthiest group report satisfaction; a poor person is 0.80 as likely as a richer person to be
  satisfied; the ratio for dissatisfaction is 2.4 (0.31/0.13), i.e. the poor are about 2.5 times
  more likely to be dissatisfied (p. 127).
  Between nations (Table V, p. 138): correlations of national income with mean SWB range 0.51-0.70
  across nine studies; mean about 0.60 (p. 136).
  Over time (Tables III, VI; pp. 140-141): SWB slopes over decades near zero in the US and Britain
  (mean 0.007 across 15 nations); cross-sectional growth-SWB correlations inconsistent (+0.49 to
  -0.24); "there is no necessary long-term relation between increases in income and higher SWB"
  (p. 141); Japan showed virtually no SWB rise despite huge growth from 1958.
  Interpretation (summary c. p. 145; Conclusions p. 161): "poverty can and does lower SWB"; once
  incomes are high by world standards additional wealth has "a very small influence"; a higher
  income "might help if we are very poor", living in a wealthy society is beneficial, and more
  income for the middle or upper class in a rich nation is unlikely to substantially bolster SWB.
  The authors favour a "rising desires" account over a pure basic-needs account (pp. 145-148).
  Sample: not applicable (review of many surveys, including World Value Survey and national panels).

  Supports the "not sufficient" half strongly (small within-nation correlations; no long-run gain
  from growth in rich nations; materialism inversely related to SWB) and supports the low-end

  of wellbeing rather than being a precondition; and "material security" is broader than the
  income measures reviewed.
